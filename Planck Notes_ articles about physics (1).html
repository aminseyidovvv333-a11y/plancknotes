Why the sky is blue Optics3 min read

Air scatters short wavelengths far more strongly than long ones.

Sunlight looks white but contains every color. As it passes through the atmosphere, molecules of nitrogen and oxygen scatter it in all directions. This is Rayleigh scattering, and its strength grows as 1/λ⁴, so blue light scatters roughly five to six times more than red.

Violet scatters even more, but the Sun emits less of it, the upper atmosphere absorbs some, and our eyes respond weakly to it. The result looks blue. At sunset, light travels through much more air, the blue is scattered away from your line of sight, and reds and oranges remain.

Entropy and the arrow of time Thermodynamics4 min read

Why eggs break but never unbreak, and what that says about the past.

The laws of motion work the same forwards and backwards, yet we only ever see time run one way. The answer lies in counting. Boltzmann defined entropy as S = k ln W, where W is the number of microscopic arrangements that look the same on the large scale.

A broken egg has vastly more arrangements than an intact one, so systems drift toward it. The arrow of time is therefore tied to a puzzle about the beginning: the early universe must have started in a state of very low entropy.

Time dilation you can measure Relativity4 min read

Cosmic-ray muons reach the ground only because their clocks run slow.

Muons are created about 15 kilometres up when cosmic rays hit the atmosphere. At rest a muon lives around 2.2 microseconds, enough to travel only a few hundred metres even at nearly the speed of light.

Yet many reach the ground. From our frame, the muon’s internal clock runs slow by the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²), extending its lifetime many times over. Detectors at sea level count the survivors, and the numbers match Einstein’s prediction.

The double-slit experiment Quantum5 min read

Single electrons build an interference pattern, one dot at a time.

Fire electrons at a barrier with two narrow slits and record where each lands. Even when they are sent one at a time, the dots gradually form bands of high and low density, the signature of waves interfering.

If you add a detector to learn which slit each electron used, the bands disappear. The experiment shows that quantum objects are described by probability amplitudes that add together, and that gaining which-path information changes the outcome.

Why a spinning top does not fall Mechanics3 min read

Gravity turns the spin axis sideways instead of tipping the top over.

A tilted top feels a torque from gravity about its contact point. For a non-spinning object that torque makes it fall. For a spinning one, torque changes the angular momentum vector, and the change points sideways, perpendicular to the tilt.

The axis therefore sweeps slowly around the vertical, a motion called precession. The faster the spin, the slower the precession. Friction gradually reduces the spin, and when it is low enough the top wobbles and falls.

How a neutron star holds together Astrophysics4 min read

Quantum rules about identical particles resist the crush of gravity.

When a massive star runs out of fuel, its core collapses. If the core is heavy enough, protons and electrons are squeezed into neutrons, leaving an object more massive than the Sun but only about 20 kilometres across.

What stops further collapse is neutron degeneracy pressure. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids identical neutrons from sharing the same quantum state, so squeezing them together forces them into higher energies, which pushes back. Beyond a limit, even this fails and a black hole forms.
